Deciding What to Accept Automatically

Three of the four shipped connectors offer some way to accept inbound collaboration requests without an agent, and the fourth deliberately does not. They disagree completely about when that is a good idea — and the disagreement is worth understanding, because accepting a case is not a routing step. It is a promise to another company.

The reframe

Acceptance Is a Commitment, Not a Triage Step

It is tempting to read /approval as "route this to my team". It is not. Accepting a collaboration request tells the partner that an engineer is engaged on their problem, and it satisfies the response SLA at the same moment. Those two effects pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the whole subject of this page.

Accepting does thisWhich means
Sets responded to trueThe platform stops tracking respondBy. Your SLA obligation is met — the clock is off.
Records an engineer against the caseThe partner sees a named contact and a case number, and reasonably expects work to be happening.
Moves the case out of OPENReject and Request-information are no longer offered by the shipped connectors once a case is accepted. The triage decision has been made. (Connector behaviour — the API page does not state this rule itself.)

So automatic acceptance buys guaranteed SLA compliance and spends credibility. If your rule accepts something nobody is going to work, the clock stops and the partner waits — which is a worse outcome for them than an honest breach, because a breach is visible and a false acceptance is not.

The question is therefore not "should we automate acceptance". It is "what can we assert automatically that is actually true". A rule that can verify entitlement is asserting something. A rule that accepts everything is asserting nothing except that the clock should stop.

The spectrum

Four Postures, All Shipping Today

Accept Everything

Zendesk — app setting · Salesforce — Auto Accept template

Every inbound case is accepted the moment it arrives. No condition, no lookup, no check that the request is one you can act on. Zendesk ships it as a built-in setting, off by default; Salesforce ships it as a flow template, inactive until you activate it.

Buys: the SLA can never be breached. Spends: every case is a promise, including the ones that turn out not to be yours.

Accept What Matches Existing Work

Dynamics — auto-routing

Look for one of your own cases whose number the partner quoted on the form, and relate to it. Failing that, find the customer contact by email and open a new case under their account. Either way, accept.

Buys: re-collaboration on a known issue attaches to the right place instead of opening a duplicate. Spends: says nothing about entitlement, and the email fallback accepts new work from any known contact.

Accept What You Can Verify

Salesforce — asset match, as designed in 2.13

Resolve the request to an asset you actually have a record of. On a match, create the case and accept. On no match, ask the partner for more information instead of accepting. In the 2.14 package the lookup is no longer wired in; see below.

Buys: acceptance means something checkable. Spends: needs clean asset data, and an unmatched request waits on the partner's answer while the clock runs.

Never Accept Automatically

Fin / Intercom — guardrail

Automation drafts and proposes; a person accepts, declines or asks for more information. The commitment stays with a human even when the answer is machine-generated.

Buys: no promise is made that nobody chose to make. Spends: the SLA clock runs until someone acts.

Implementations

How Each Connector Actually Does It

Zendesk: A Setting for Everything, a Trigger for Conditions

Since app v1.0.69 there are two paths, split by policy. Accepting everything is a built-in app setting — Auto-accept inbound requests, off by default — that accepts each inbound case the moment its ticket is created, with your ticket number as the case reference. It is deliberately unconditional, and toggling it takes effect only after a bundle redeploy. Accepting conditionally stays with a Zendesk trigger on the TSANet Action field, which rides the optional field-actions feature: fire on ticket creation, condition on the inbound tag, action sets TSANet Action to Accept. Both run server-side, so no agent needs to be logged in, and both are verified end to end — the partner sees the case accepted roughly fifteen seconds after submitting it.

Crucially, trigger conditions are the scoping mechanism. A condition on the partner field turns "accept everything" into "accept everything from this partner" — which moves you along the spectrum without writing anything. Cases that do not match simply stay open for normal triage.

The published guide is honest about the cost: auto-accept commits you instantly, and the triage options are skipped for auto-accepted cases. If some cases should still be looked at first, use the trigger path and put those conditions in the trigger. Setup guide →

Salesforce: Verify, Then Accept — Or Ask

The template Trigger Handler (Asset) is the most opinionated of the four, as it was built. It ships deactivated, for you to clone and activate. It fires on TSANet Case creation where direction is inbound and no local case is linked. In 2.13 it ran like this:

Get Asset                            as shipped, Asset Name = the case's email field
  └─ no match  → send the partner an information request
  └─ match     → continue
Create the Salesforce Case
Link it to the TSANet Case
Approve the collaboration request
Notify the case owner

Asking on no match is the entitlement check in its softer form. A partner escalating about something you have no record of does not become a case your team owns. It gets a question back straight away instead of a silent wait.

Three sources, three behaviours. The published guide says the no-match branch rejects. In the package source at Release-v2.13, it asks the partner for information. In 2.14 (the TSANetConnect-2.14-Release tag and main), the flow's start goes straight to creating the Salesforce Case and accepting: the asset lookup and the information-request branch are still in the file but nothing leads to them, so the activated template accepts every inbound case, like the plain trigger handler. No issue tracks the change, so treat it as unexplained rather than intended. Source-read on 2026-11-17, not run in an org. If you want the entitlement check, reconnect the start to Get Asset in your clone; if rejection is your policy, change the no-match branch; and replace the lookup either way, because matching an Asset Name against an email field is a placeholder. Details on the Salesforce page.

Salesforce also ships two simpler pieces. Auto Accept Collaboration Request is an inactive template that accepts every inbound case. Find Parent Case ships active and links an inbound case to an existing Salesforce Case whose number the partner quoted; because the trigger handlers only fire when no case is linked, a matched request is left for a person.

Dynamics: Attach to Work That Already Exists

Auto-routing is a feature toggle rather than a flow to activate, and it answers "where does this belong" before it answers "should we take this". It reads two fields from the partner's request form, Customer Case # and Customer Email, so it only works if your form has fields with exactly those names. It first looks for one of your cases whose ticket number matches the quoted case number, and relates the collaboration to it. Failing that, it finds a contact with the quoted email and, if the contact belongs to an account, opens a new case under that account. In both branches it then approves the collaboration with "An engineer has been assigned and will contact you."

So this is acceptance, not just routing. Matching on your own case number is what makes re-collaboration on a continuing issue attach to the same case rather than opening a duplicate. The email fallback goes further: it accepts any request from a known customer contact, which is new work. In the solution source, the flow ships active and its toggle, ap_AutoRoutingofnewCase, defaults to yes; check your installed solution rather than assuming it is off. Source-read, not checked against an installed v2.14.0.0.

Dynamics also has the first automatic rejection in any connector, though not a release: since 2026-11-11 on main, the end-to-end encryption PoC rejects a received encrypted case that fails verification. It is covered on the end-to-end encryption topic.

Fin / Intercom: Draft Automatically, Commit Deliberately

The gateway-class connector takes the opposite position and states the reasoning explicitly: accepting, declining and requesting information are contractual commitments to another vendor, made under a service-level clock. Automation composes the answer; a person makes the commitment.

There is a second reason specific to AI-assisted flows. Partner escalations carry end-customer logs and text pasted in verbatim — untrusted content arriving at a system that would then reply under your company's name. Keeping a human on the commitment is also a containment boundary.

Designing yours

Building a Rule That Asserts Something True

The Salesforce flow, as designed in 2.13, is the useful template even on other platforms, because its shape generalises: resolve an identifier, branch on whether you found it, and decide what no match means. What varies is the identifier.

Match onAssertsFails when
Asset or serial numberWe support this specific thingAsset records are stale or the partner quotes a different identifier
Entitlement or contractThis customer is covered right nowCoverage lives in a system the connector cannot reach
Partner identityWe have an arrangement with this companySays nothing about the specific request
Existing case numberWe are already working this issueFirst contact on a new issue never matches
Product or componentThis is a thing our team ownsField is free text and partners fill it inconsistently

Two design rules worth adopting whichever identifier you pick:

  • Decide what "no match" means before you build. There are three answers. Rejecting is a real answer, and kinder than silence, but only if your matching is good enough that a rejection is usually correct. Asking for information, which is what the Salesforce template did in 2.13, is safer when the partner may just have quoted a different identifier. Leaving the case open for a human is the fallback when you are unsure. Rejecting wrongly is worse than not automating.
  • Start scoped, then widen. One partner, or one product line, with everything else falling through to normal triage. Every connector that automates acceptance supports a conditional form, and it converts a policy question into an observation: run it narrow for a month and count how often the rule was right.

Failure modes

What Goes Wrong, in the Order You Will Meet It

  • The silent accept. A rule accepts a case that lands in a queue nobody watches. The clock is off, so nothing escalates, and the partner waits on an engineer who was never assigned. This is the failure that auto-accept exists to prevent and the one it most often causes.
  • The confident reject. Matching runs against stale reference data and declines a request that was perfectly valid. The partner has to re-submit, and now believes your integration is unreliable. Audit your rejections before you trust them — they are the ones nobody complains about internally.
  • Accepted, but nothing was created. The approval call succeeds and the local case creation fails after it. The partner sees acceptance; you have no record. Order the steps so the local record exists first where the platform allows it, and treat a post-acceptance failure as an alert rather than a log line.
  • Status divergence. Your side auto-accepts and shows a case as owned, while the partner's connector shows only "Accepted" with no engineer name they recognise. Both are technically right. Agree with your frequent partners what acceptance means operationally, because the API does not define it for you.
  • The rule outliving its reason. A scoped trigger added for one partner during one incident is still accepting everything from them two years later. Anything that commits automatically deserves a review date.
If you are unsure where to start: leave acceptance manual until you have a matching rule you would defend to a partner, and check that nothing is already accepting for you. On Dynamics, auto-routing accepts as well as routes, and may be on by default; on Salesforce 2.14, an activated asset template accepts everything.
